## Meaning

Vesta is the brightest asteroid, at times just visible to the naked eye, a body of about 525 kilometres between Mars and Jupiter. It goes round the Sun in about 3.6 years; seen from the Earth it stays from about two to nine and a half months in a sign, and its retrograde periods last about three months, roughly every sixteen or seventeen months.

It bears the name of the Roman goddess of the hearth, the Greek Hestia, whose sacred fire in Rome was kept burning by the Vestal priestesses. Astrologers read it as focus and devotion: what someone dedicates themselves to, how they protect their concentration, the work or practice that feels sacred, and the private space needed to do it well.

Its gifts are dedication, steadiness and a capacity for deep work. Its difficulties are withdrawal, a single purpose that crowds out the rest of life, and keeping the flame for others while forgetting one’s own warmth.

## In practice

- **In close aspect** to the [[sun]], Vesta can look like years spent perfecting one craft, a strict morning routine and working time guarded from interruptions.
- **Its house** shows where devotion goes: in the 6th, daily work done with care; in the 9th, study or a spiritual practice.
- **Its sign** describes the style of focus, from Aries’s bursts of commitment to Virgo’s patient attention to detail.
- **With Venus or Mars** in close aspect, the balance between closeness to others and time kept for one’s own work often becomes a central question.

## Origin and history

Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers found Vesta on 29 March 1807, five years after Pallas, and left its naming to Carl Friedrich Gauss. The space probe Dawn orbited it in 2011 and 2012 before going on to Ceres. Vesta entered astrology with the other first asteroids in the 1970s, after Eleanor Bach’s ephemeris of 1973, and Demetra George’s Asteroid Goddesses of 1986 shaped its modern reading. Traditional astrology, Indian and Chinese astrology and Human Design do not use it.

## How Ulune calculates it

Ulune takes Vesta from the Swiss Ephemeris’s asteroid files, which cover 600 BC to AD 2400, as the planets’ do. It is marked retrograde when its daily motion runs backwards and is aspected with the same orbs as the planets. Ulune gives it no dignity, leaves it out of aspect patterns, and the calendar does not date its transits.
