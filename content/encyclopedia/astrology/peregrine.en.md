## Meaning

A planet is peregrine when it holds none of the five essential dignities where it stands. It is in neither its [[domicile]] nor its [[exaltation]]. It rules neither the [[triplicity]], nor the [[term]], nor the [[face]] of its degree. The word comes from the Latin *peregrinus*, a foreigner or traveller, the root of pilgrim.

Tradition reads a peregrine planet as a stranger in the place. It has no house, no allies and no small corner of its own there. It must rely on its own resources, or on the goodwill of the planets that do rule the place. Older authors could read it as unsettled, wandering, or acting without a fixed aim.

Peregrine is not the same as debilitated. A planet in [[detriment]] or [[fall]] stands somewhere hostile; a peregrine planet stands somewhere neutral, where nobody knows it. William Lilly counts it as a weakness all the same, worth minus five points, as much as detriment.

Many astrologers today see another side. A planet without fixed ties can be free, adaptable and open to influence. It takes its colour from what it meets: its aspects, its house and the planet that hosts it.

## In practice

- **Look to the host.** A peregrine planet depends on its [[dispositor]], the ruler of its sign. When that ruler is strong and in good contact with it, the guest is well looked after.
- **Reception helps.** A peregrine planet in [[reception|mutual reception]] with another planet has a partner where it stands, and tradition reads it as much relieved.
- **Accidental strength still counts.** A peregrine planet on an angle, or swift and well aspected, can be prominent and effective. Essential dignity describes its quality, not how much it shows.
- **Example.** The Sun at 15° Gemini in a day chart is peregrine: Gemini is Mercury’s sign, Saturn rules air by day, Venus the term, and Mars the face.

## Across traditions

- **Indian astrology** grades a planet by the sign it stands in. The scale runs from exalted and own sign, through the signs of friends, neutrals and enemies, down to debilitation. A peregrine planet is closest to a planet in a neutral sign.
- **Modern psychological astrology** seldom uses the word. It often reads an unsupported planet as a function that works on its own and asks to be developed consciously.

## Origin and history

Ptolemy already describes all five kinds of rulership in the *Tetrabiblos*: houses, exaltations, triangles, terms and faces. In medieval Latin astrology, the word *peregrinus* came to name a planet holding none of them.

Lilly, in *Christian Astrology* (1647), defines a planet as peregrine when it has no essential dignity in its place. He scores it minus five in his table of debilities, beside detriment and fall.

## How Ulune calculates it

Ulune decides peregrine for the seven traditional planets, with the Egyptian terms, the Chaldean faces and the Dorothean triplicities. A planet is peregrine when it holds no dignity and also no debility. It is not in its domicile, exaltation, term or face, nor the triplicity ruler of the chart’s sect. Nor is it in its detriment or fall.

Two conventions follow. A planet that is only the out-of-sect or participating triplicity ruler still counts as peregrine, because that role is shown but not scored. A planet in detriment or fall is never also called peregrine, even with no dignity; it keeps its −5 or −4 alone.

A peregrine planet scores −5. The Dignities table shows the single word “peregrine” for it, and the wheel’s dignity overlay marks it with a P. Without a birth time, the label is marked ~ when the sect, or a crossing into another term or face during the day, could change it.
