## Meaning

Aldebaran is the bright orange-red star in the face of Taurus, the Bull’s eye. Its name comes from the Arabic *al-dabarān*, “the follower”, because it rises after the Pleiades and seems to chase them across the sky. It shines at about magnitude 0.9, among the brightest stars of the night, and lies some 65 light-years away.

Its degree was 9°47′ Gemini in 2000. Like every star, it moves forward about a degree in 72 years, so it stands near 10° Gemini in the 2020s. It lies about 5½° south of the ecliptic, close enough for the Moon to pass in front of it in some years.

Ptolemy compares the bright star of the Hyades to [[mars]]. Tradition reads Aldebaran as courage, drive and a strong sense of honour: the energy to lead, to speak up and to stand firm. Its difficult side is rashness and a quick temper. Modern writers often link it with success that lasts as long as one keeps one’s integrity.

## In practice

- **On the Sun or the Midheaven**, Aldebaran is read as a wish to be seen for one’s courage and principles. A public role then asks for steady ethics.
- **On the Moon**, it is read as strong feelings, quickly roused, and loyalty fiercely held.
- **On Mars**, the two voices agree: tradition reads boldness, energy and a need to act, best given a worthy cause.
- **By sign**, it stands in early [[gemini]] in the tropical zodiac, though it belongs to the constellation [[taurus]]. A Taurus planet does not meet it.

## Across traditions

- **Indian astrology** names the lunar mansion of Aldebaran Rohini, “the red one”. In myth, Rohini was the favourite of the Moon’s 27 wives, the nakshatras.
- **Chinese astronomy** calls it 毕宿五 Bìxiùwǔ, the fifth star of the Net lodge.
- **The four royal stars.** Modern books often call Aldebaran, [[regulus]], [[antares]] and [[fomalhaut]] the four royal stars, or watchers of the sky. They credit the idea to ancient Persia. The grouping is popular, but its ancient basis is debated. The stars do lie roughly a quarter of the zodiac apart. Around 3000 BC, Aldebaran and Antares stood close to the equinoxes, Regulus and Fomalhaut within about ten degrees of the solstices.

## Origin and history

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* lists Aldebaran as the bright, reddish star of the Hyades, on the Bull’s southern eye. In the *Tetrabiblos* he gives it the nature of Mars. Medieval magic counted it among the fifteen Behenian stars, and Agrippa lists it in his *De occulta philosophia*. Vivian Robson, in 1923, gathered the old readings: honour, eloquence, integrity and courage. Bernadette Brady, in 1998, tied the star to integrity and to the Bull’s myth.
